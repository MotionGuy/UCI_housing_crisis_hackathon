Project context: "Clear It" lane, UC Irvine AI Impact Lab housing hackathon. Team of 4, about 4 hours to build and demo.

The problem. In Orange County, a faster path to building housing already exists on paper: build on land that is already zoned correctly, use a ministerial approval (if the project meets the written rules and uses state-approved plans, the city must approve it, with no hearing and no discretion), and the timeline can drop from about four years to about two. In practice almost nobody uses this path, because each of OC's 34 cities has its own forms, fees and checklists, cities often misread state law (the state housing department, HCD, regularly sends letters correcting them), and most applications come back incomplete or need several rounds of corrections. The people stuck in this process are nonprofit project managers, small builders, homeowners adding an ADU, and city permit techs who want to get it right. The question we are answering: how do we make the fast path easy enough that it becomes the normal path instead of the rare one?

Our solution. We are building "TurboTax for ADU permits" in Orange County, starting with Newport Beach because it has the strongest public documentation. It is applicant-facing. The goal is not to explain the rules but to get the first submission right. Newport's own ADU guidebook says applications typically need 2 to 3 review rounds of 10 to 20 business days each, which is roughly 1 to 3 months of cycle time, and every extra round is where applicants lose time and money. A tool that moves the typical project to a single review cycle is the entire value proposition. The user answers step-by-step questions about their lot and project, and the tool tells them which forms they need, which departments must sign off, what fees apply, and which problems in their application would likely trigger a correction letter, with a plain-English fix for each one, before they submit.

How it should work. Compliance checks are rules written from Newport's ADU submittal checklist and from current state ADU law (Government Code 66310 through 66342; the old section 65852.2 was renumbered, so flag any document that still cites it). Every rule and check must cite its source. The AI is a helper, not the judge: it guides the intake questions, explains flagged issues in plain language, and can read documents the user uploads, but pass/fail decisions come from the written rules. Every number we show (hours saved, money saved, correction rates such as the claim that over 90% of applications are deemed incomplete) must either have a cited source or be clearly labeled as an assumption. We cannot prove we reduce correction letters in 4 hours, so the demo will run a realistic sample application with common mistakes planted in it and show the tool catching what a plan checker would have flagged. This is not legal advice, and the app should say so.

Work to cover. (1) Requirements: the forms, checklist items, fees and sign-off departments for a Newport Beach ADU, plus the state limits on what the city may require, stored as structured data with sources. (2) Correction prevention, which is the core of the product: the most common reasons ADU applications get sent back, each turned into a pre-submission check tied to a requirement. (3) The app: a step-by-step intake in the existing Next.js project, ending in a results page showing forms needed, issues found, who reviews the application, and a ready-to-submit status. (4) Pitch and research: competitors (for example Blitz AI; verify what each one actually does and how we differ), how often ADU law and city rules change and so how often our data must be updated, estimates of hours and money saved per avoided correction round (labeled as estimates), and the demo script. Research tasks should be time-boxed to about 20 minutes each, since they feed the pitch, not the build.

Priorities. Correction-prevention checks matter most; if time runs short, cut AI chat features, fee details and visual polish before cutting checks. One city done well beats many cities done badly. Feature work stops about an hour before the deadline so the team can integrate and rehearse a demo that runs start to finish. Expect judges to ask how this fits "four years into two": our answer is that plan check is a large share of a homeowner ADU timeline, and the same rules engine extends to SB 9 and small multifamily projects, where each correction round costs more.
